## Program Overview

### Credential
**Pilates & Mommy Certified Instructor (PMCI)**

### Who This Program Is For
This certification is designed for professionals who hold an existing Pilates instructor credential and wish to add a maternal and postnatal specialty. Students must hold a current Pilates certification from a recognized training organization prior to enrollment.

You do not need a background in obstetrics, pediatrics, or early childhood education to begin this program — those foundations are built here. What you bring is your Pilates knowledge, your professionalism, and your commitment to meeting mothers in one of the most transformative seasons of their lives.

### Format
Self-paced hybrid learning: video instruction, written content, and practical assignments. Students move through twelve sequential modules and complete a written examination and video practicum submission to earn the PMCI credential.

### Focus
**Postnatal — birth through 3 years with baby present.**

This is not a prenatal program. It is designed specifically for the postpartum period and for the Mommy & Me class format, in which mothers and their babies or toddlers move together in a structured, developmentally-informed environment.

### Upon Completion
Graduates receive:
- The **Pilates & Mommy Certified Instructor (PMCI)** credential
- A physical **Instructor Kit** shipped to the graduate's address
- Access to the instructor community and continuing education pathway

## The Class Format

### Overview
**Mommy & Me Pilates** is a group fitness experience designed for mothers with babies and toddlers ages 3 months through 3 years. The class integrates a full Pilates mat sequence with developmentally-appropriate infant and toddler activities, sensory play, baby massage, and a guided closing meditation.

The format was created by Dr. Niia Bishop in 2009 under the original name **MAMiLATES**, and has been refined over more than fifteen years of direct instruction.

### Class Setup
Mats are arranged in a circle. Babies and toddlers play in the center of the circle on soft flooring with toys from the Instructor Kit. The circle creates visual connection between all participants, allows the instructor to cue while maintaining eye contact with every dyad, and establishes the parachute — which defines the perimeter — as a shared class boundary and prop.

### Duration
- **Full format:** 90 minutes
- **Core-only format:** 60 minutes (omits parachute, scarf activities, and extended cool-down)

### Music
Contemporary, uptempo remixes of nursery rhymes and children's songs with a gym and movement feel. Music should be energizing without being abrasive, familiar without being tedious. Licensing and curation are addressed in Module 10.

### Baby as Prop
Where age and developmental stage are appropriate, the baby is used as a resistance prop — in chest presses, bicep curls, overhead lifts, and supported squats. All baby-as-prop exercises are gated by weight and mobility milestones covered in Module 8.

### Closing Ritual
Every session ends with a guided baby massage sequence narrated by the instructor, followed by a lullaby or quiet song and a brief meditation. This is not optional or decorative — it is a clinically-grounded closing arc that serves infant development, maternal nervous system regulation, and attachment. The developmental rationale for each massage stroke is covered in Module 8.

---

### Full 90-Minute Session Arc

| Time | Duration | Segment | Notes |
|------|----------|---------|-------|
| 0:00 | 10 min | **Arrival and Welcome** | Setup, greetings, "Who's Here Today" song (Farmer in the Dell adapted with each child's name) |
| 0:10 | 10 min | **Infant Massage Intro + Mom Neck/Shoulder Release** | Light touch massage introduction for babies; targeted neck and shoulder release for moms before loading |
| 0:20 | 30 min | **Full Mat Pilates Sequence** | See exercise list below |
| 0:50 | 10 min | **Active Play Break** | Freeze dance, Airplane Song, bubbles |
| 1:00 | 10 min | **Parachute** | Group parachute activities (see Appendix H) |
| 1:10 | 10 min | **Baby Massage + Pat-a-Cake Send-Off** | Instructor narrates strokes and developmental purpose; Pat-a-Cake closes the active segment |
| 1:20 | 10 min | **Lullaby, Closing Meditation, Goodbye** | Quiet transition; instructor-led breathing and body scan for moms while babies settle |

### Full Mat Pilates Sequence (0:20–0:50)

The following exercises are sequenced for the postpartum body. All are performed on the mat. Modifications for each movement are detailed in Module 7.

1. Squats with bicep curl (baby held in arms or in carrier for modified version)
2. Chest press (baby placed on shins or held at chest height)
3. Lunges holding baby (bilateral)
4. Pelvic tilts (supine)
5. Bridges
6. Tabletop series (dead bug progressions, breath-coordinated)
7. Roll-ups (modified for diastasis — see Module 3)
8. The Hundred (breath-gated entry)
9. The Saw
10. Leg circles
11. Single leg stretch
12. Criss cross (modified approach for postpartum oblique recruitment)
13. Swan (prone extension — baby on mat in front of mom)
14. Quadruped (bird-dog)
15. Plank with kisses (forearm or full plank; baby placed on mat below mom's face for engagement)
16. Side series (side-lying leg work)

## The Space

### Philosophy
The Mommy & Me environment must be simultaneously suitable for a Pilates practice and safe for infants and toddlers. These are not competing requirements — they are design constraints that, when respected, create a distinctive and premium experience. The space communicates to families before a single instruction is given that this is a thoughtful, professionally managed environment.

### Dr. Bishop's Home Base
**The Smart Sanctuary** — Dr. Bishop's personal studio in Columbia, SC — serves as the prototype Pilates & Mommy space and the model for certified instructors designing their own environments.

### Ideal Venue Types
- Dance studios (sprung flooring, mirrors optional but useful)
- Yoga studios (soft flooring, low-stimulation aesthetic)
- Community centers (accessible, familiar to families)
- Outdoor spaces (shaded, weather-appropriate, seasonal programming)
- Home studios (low overhead, intimate, ideal for small cohorts)

### Childproofing Requirements
All instructors are responsible for ensuring their teaching space meets the following minimums before the first session:

- Electrical outlet covers on all accessible outlets
- Door stoppers or door pinch guards on all doors within child reach
- Soft, non-slip flooring throughout the class area (no concrete, tile, or hardwood without mats)
- No exposed Pilates apparatus, weights, or resistance equipment within child access range
- No open shelving with hard objects at infant/toddler height
- Designated clear center zone for infant/toddler play area

A complete **Childproofing Checklist for Rental Spaces** is included in Appendix K.

### The Bolster

The Bolster is the signature clinical tool of the Pilates & Mommy program and is both a component of the Instructor Kit and a standalone postpartum recovery product sold direct-to-consumer.

**What it is:** A cylindrical, firm-but-yielding foam bolster engineered to support the body weight of an adult in prone position.

**What it does:** Used in prone positioning, the Bolster provides gentle proprioceptive feedback to the anterior abdominal wall, encouraging visceral and abdominal repositioning in the postpartum period. The abdominal organs shift significantly during pregnancy and require time and intentional positioning to return to their pre-pregnancy orientation. The Bolster supports this process non-invasively.

**Origin:** The technique was first described to Dr. Bishop by her midwife during her own postpartum recovery — the original version was a rolled pillow secured with duct tape. The Bolster is the productized version of that protocol.

**How it is used in class:** The Bolster is introduced in the prone segment of the mat sequence (swan/cobra prep) and can be offered to students who have cleared contraindications. Full usage protocol is detailed in Module 6.

**As a DTC product:** The Bolster is also sold as a standalone postpartum recovery tool to women who are not enrolled in a class, through the Pilates & Mommy direct-to-consumer channel.

## Module 1: The Postpartum Body — A Systems Overview

### Learning Objectives
By the end of this module, the student will be able to:
- Identify the primary hormonal shifts of the postpartum period and their implications for movement
- Describe the musculoskeletal adaptations that persist after birth
- Recognize common postpartum complaints that affect exercise tolerance and participation

### Hormonal Changes Post-Birth

The hormonal landscape of the postpartum period is as dramatic as any in human physiology. Within hours of delivery, the placenta — which had been producing progesterone, estrogen, and human placental lactogen throughout pregnancy — is expelled. This triggers a cascade of rapid hormonal change that continues for months.

**Relaxin** remains elevated for the duration of breastfeeding and for up to twelve months postpartum. Originally produced to allow pelvic ligaments to widen for birth, relaxin does not selectively target pelvic tissue — it affects all connective tissue throughout the body. This means joints throughout the kinetic chain are hypermobile and susceptible to injury from improper loading. The Pilates instructor must understand that postpartum joint hypermobility is not flexibility — it is ligamentous laxity, and it requires a more conservative, stability-first movement approach.

**Estrogen** drops precipitously after delivery, particularly in breastfeeding women. Low estrogen contributes to vaginal dryness, mood instability, fatigue, and impaired collagen synthesis — affecting the very connective tissue being asked to repair and reorganize.

**Oxytocin** surges during breastfeeding and skin-to-skin contact, producing the bonding and calm association most people know it for. In the movement context, oxytocin also stimulates uterine contractions in the early postpartum period — instructors should be aware that exercise intensity can influence these contractions and should monitor students for increased lochia (postpartum bleeding) as a sign of overexertion.

**Prolactin**, the hormone of milk production, suppresses ovulation and contributes to continued ligament laxity while the mother is nursing. Students who are actively breastfeeding are operating under a different hormonal profile than those who have weaned — this matters for joint loading, hydration requirements, and energy availability.

### Musculoskeletal Adaptations

The musculoskeletal changes of the postpartum period do not resolve at six weeks. Many persist for six months to a year or longer, and some — particularly in women who experienced significant diastasis recti or pelvic floor dysfunction — require targeted rehabilitation before conventional Pilates progressions are appropriate.

Key adaptations to understand:

- **Lumbar lordosis** increased during pregnancy to shift the center of gravity forward. Postpartum, the abdominals are too weak to counteract this curve, leading to persistent hyperlordosis and low back pain.
- **Thoracic kyphosis** is worsened by nursing posture, baby-carrying, and forward head position — often dramatically so.
- **Hip abductor and external rotator weakness** develops from altered gait mechanics during pregnancy. The hip shift common in pregnancy (weight-bearing into one hip) becomes a habitual postural pattern postpartum.
- **Gluteal amnesia** — inhibition of the glute max and glute med — is common postpartum, partly from prolonged sitting during nursing and recovery, partly from altered movement patterns during pregnancy.
- **Scapular instability** from the sustained forward reach of nursing and baby-holding compromises posterior shoulder and rotator cuff integrity.

### Common Complaints and Their Movement Implications

| Complaint | Mechanism | Movement Implication |
|-----------|-----------|---------------------|
| Fatigue | Sleep deprivation, hormonal disruption, caloric demand of lactation | Reduce intensity expectations; shorter work sets, longer recovery |
| Joint hypermobility | Relaxin-mediated ligament laxity | Avoid end-range loading; emphasize stability over flexibility |
| Breast engorgement | Milk production, lymphatic congestion | Prone positioning may be uncomfortable; modify during engorgement |
| Wrist pain (DeQuervain's tenosynovitis) | Repetitive infant-lifting mechanics, relaxin | Modify weight-bearing on wrists; use fists or forearms in plank |
| Low back pain | Lumbar hyperlordosis, gluteal inhibition, weakened TVA | Core rehabilitation sequencing (Module 7) |
| Pelvic girdle pain | Symphysis pubis dysfunction, SI joint instability | Load bilaterally; avoid single-leg loading until cleared |
| Neck/shoulder pain | Nursing posture, forward head, baby-holding | Priority anterior chain release (Module 6) |

## Module 2: Pelvic Floor Anatomy and Function

### Learning Objectives
By the end of this module, the student will be able to:
- Identify the major muscles of the pelvic floor and their functional roles
- Distinguish between hypertonic and hypotonic pelvic floor dysfunction
- Apply basic screening language to identify students who need referral to a pelvic floor physical therapist

### Note to Students
This module is educational — it is not clinical training. Pilates & Mommy Certified Instructors do not diagnose, treat, or rehabilitate pelvic floor dysfunction. This module gives you the vocabulary, awareness, and referral criteria to work safely with postpartum women and to be a knowledgeable collaborator with pelvic floor physical therapists in your community.

### Anatomy

The pelvic floor is a hammock of muscle and connective tissue that spans the base of the pelvis, from the pubic bone anteriorly to the coccyx posteriorly, and from one sit bone to the other laterally. It supports the pelvic organs (bladder, uterus, rectum), contributes to continence, assists in sexual function, and plays an essential role in intra-abdominal pressure management — which makes it central to any core rehabilitation work.

**Primary muscles:**

- **Levator ani** — the largest and most functionally significant muscle group. Composed of:
  - *Pubococcygeus* — runs from pubic bone to coccyx; primary sphincter control
  - *Puborectalis* — forms the anorectal angle; critical for defecation control
  - *Iliococcygeus* — lateral component; supports pelvic organ position
- **Coccygeus (ischiococcygeus)** — posterior pelvic floor; connects ischial spine to coccyx; often implicated in coccydynia (tailbone pain)
- **Urogenital triangle** muscles — superficial layer including the bulbospongiosus and ischiocavernosus; contribute to urethral and vaginal sphincter function

**The pelvic floor in postpartum context:**

Vaginal birth subjects the levator ani to significant lengthening — often exceeding the muscle's safe stretch capacity. Tearing, episiotomy, and instrumental delivery (forceps or vacuum) add additional tissue disruption. Cesarean delivery preserves the pelvic floor from the stretch of vaginal birth but does not exempt the patient from pelvic floor dysfunction — the weight of pregnancy, altered posture, and postoperative scar tissue all contribute.

### Pelvic Floor Dysfunction: Hypertonic vs. Hypotonic

The common assumption is that postpartum women have weak (hypotonic) pelvic floors. This is frequently true — but it is not universally true, and treating a hypertonic pelvic floor with strengthening exercises causes harm.

| | Hypertonic (Overactive) | Hypotonic (Underactive) |
|--|------------------------|------------------------|
| **What it is** | Pelvic floor muscles that are too tight, unable to fully relax | Pelvic floor muscles that are too weak, unable to generate adequate contraction |
| **Common symptoms** | Pelvic pain, pain with intercourse (dyspareunia), difficulty inserting a tampon, constipation, urgency without leaking | Stress urinary incontinence (leaking with cough, sneeze, jump), prolapse sensation ("heaviness"), reduced sensation |
| **Kegel appropriate?** | No — Kegels worsen hypertonicity | Yes, as part of progressive loading |
| **What helps** | Diaphragmatic breathing, relaxation, pelvic floor PT | Progressive core and pelvic floor strengthening (Module 7) |

**As an instructor, you must ask.** The intake form (Appendix A) includes pelvic floor symptom screening. When a student reports any of the following, use the referral language in the next section:
- Leaking urine with exercise, sneezing, coughing, or laughing
- Pelvic pain, pressure, or heaviness
- Pain with intercourse
- Inability to control urgency (urge incontinence)
- Feeling of something protruding from the vagina

### Referral Language and Criteria

Instructors are not diagnosticians. When a student discloses pelvic floor symptoms, the appropriate response is warm, non-alarmist referral.

**Referral language:**

> *"What you're describing is really common after birth, and it's also really treatable. There's a specialist called a pelvic floor physical therapist — they work specifically with these muscles in a way that no other provider does. I'd love to connect you with someone local if you don't already have one. In the meantime, we'll make sure everything we do in class supports your floor rather than stressing it."*

**When to pause exercise and refer urgently:**
- Pelvic organ prolapse (symptomatic or diagnosed)
- Active pelvic pain (more than mild during exercise)
- Any increase in pain, pressure, or bleeding during or after class

## Module 3: Diastasis Recti — Assessment and Rehabilitation

### Learning Objectives
By the end of this module, the student will be able to:
- Explain what diastasis recti is and how it develops
- Guide a client through a basic self-assessment
- Identify contraindicated exercises and provide appropriate modifications
- Determine when a client is ready to return to full Pilates repertoire

### What Is Diastasis Recti?

Diastasis recti (DR) is the separation of the two bellies of the rectus abdominis at the linea alba — the fibrous connective tissue that runs vertically down the midline of the abdomen. During pregnancy, the growing uterus pushes the abdominal wall outward, and the linea alba widens to accommodate. After birth, this tissue must reapproximate — but in many women, it does not fully close without targeted intervention.

DR is present in the majority of women at 35 weeks of pregnancy, and studies suggest that at six months postpartum, approximately 39% still have clinically significant separation. It is not purely a cosmetic concern — a wide or functionally compromised linea alba impairs force transfer through the core, contributes to low back and pelvic pain, and limits the effectiveness of traditional abdominal exercise.

### The Self-Test Protocol (Finger-Width Test)

Instructors may guide students through this self-assessment as part of the intake process. It is not a clinical measurement but provides useful baseline information.

**Instructions:**
1. Lie supine with knees bent, feet flat on the floor.
2. Place two fingers horizontally across the midline at the level of the navel.
3. Slowly perform a small curl-up (just lifting the head and shoulder blades off the mat) while pressing the fingers gently into the abdomen.
4. Note: (a) how many fingers fit in the gap between the muscle bellies, and (b) whether there is "give" or tension under the fingers (tension indicates functional linea alba; absence of tension indicates functional compromise regardless of gap width).

**Interpretation:**
- 1 finger width or less = within normal range
- 2 finger widths = monitor; begin rehabilitation approach
- 2+ finger widths with no tension under fingers = refer to pelvic floor PT or women's health PT before progressing loading

**Important note:** Gap width alone is no longer considered the only meaningful measure. Tension and function of the linea alba matter as much as — sometimes more than — the width of the separation.

### Six Tips for Managing Diastasis Recti in Class

1. **Cue the exhale on exertion.** The exhale creates natural intra-abdominal pressure management. Never allow breath-holding during effort.
2. **Prioritize TVA activation before loading.** The transversus abdominis is the deepest abdominal layer and the primary corset for the linea alba. Its activation must be established before any rectus-dominant work.
3. **Avoid coning and doming.** Watch the midline during any abdominal exercise. If the abdomen visibly peaks or tents at the midline during an exercise, that exercise is too advanced for that student.
4. **Modify, not omit.** Students with DR can still do meaningful core work — they simply follow a modified progression (see Module 7).
5. **Educate on ADLs.** Getting out of bed (log-roll, do not sit straight up), lifting mechanics, coughing, and sneezing posture — these are daily loading events that must be managed consciously during rehabilitation.
6. **Track progress.** Re-assess every four to six weeks. Note changes in gap width and, more importantly, in functional tension and strength.

### Three Best Exercises for Diastasis Recti

1. **Diaphragmatic breath with TVA activation (90-90 position):** Foundational. Back on mat, legs at 90/90 or feet on floor. Breathe in to expand ribcage and abdomen laterally. On the exhale, gently draw the lower abdomen ("hip bones toward each other, navel to spine without compression"). Hold TVA lightly while breathing continues. This is the basis of all DR-safe core work.

2. **Dead bug (modified):** Supine, 90-90 leg position, arms extended to ceiling. Maintain TVA activation as established above. Lower one arm overhead and the opposite leg toward the floor while exhaling, return on the inhale. Monitor midline for coning. This is a cross-body coordination exercise that loads the anterior chain without compressing the linea alba.

3. **Heel slides:** Supine, knees bent. Maintain TVA. Exhale and slowly slide one heel along the mat to extension; inhale to return. Bilateral progression when single-leg sliding is controlled. This is an accessible early-stage exercise that trains pelvic stability under limb load.

### Contraindicated Movements for Active Diastasis

Do not program the following for students with unresolved DR or until cleared:

- Full sit-ups and crunches (rectus-dominant, dome-producing)
- Double leg lowering from supine
- Heavy bilateral overhead pressing
- Exercises with sustained intra-abdominal pressure (heavy resistance, Valsalva maneuver)
- High-impact jumping before functional midline tension is restored

### Clearance Criteria

A student may be cleared to return to full Pilates repertoire — including sit-ups, double-leg work, and traditional Hundred progressions — when:
- Midline gap is 2 finger widths or less AND tension is present under the fingers during curl-up
- No coning or doming is observed during modified abdominal exercises
- Student reports no pain, pressure, or leaking during current program
- If referred to pelvic floor or women's health PT, clearance from that provider is preferred

## Module 4: Cesarean Recovery

### Learning Objectives
By the end of this module, the student will be able to:
- Describe the tissue layers involved in a cesarean section and the implications for healing
- Communicate appropriately with students about return-to-movement timelines
- Apply movement contraindications specific to C-section recovery
- Understand the instructor's role in scar tissue awareness

### Understanding the Cesarean Incision

A cesarean section is a major abdominal surgery. The incision typically passes through seven layers of tissue: skin, subcutaneous fat, fascia (Scarpa's), the rectus muscle sheath, the rectus muscle bellies (separated, not cut), the peritoneum, and the uterus. Each layer heals on its own timeline, and healing is not linear.

The superficial (skin and subcutaneous) incision heals in four to six weeks. The deeper fascial and uterine layers take twelve weeks or more. The scar tissue that forms in the interim continues to remodel for up to two years, and — critically — the fascial scar can adhere to underlying structures, creating restrictions that affect posture, core function, and movement patterns well beyond the visible incision line.

### Scar Tissue Management

Scar mobilization is typically initiated by a physical therapist or midwife around six to eight weeks postpartum, once the incision is fully closed and pain-free at the surface. This is outside the scope of a Pilates instructor. However, instructors should:

- Be aware that a student with a C-section scar may have internal restrictions that limit prone positioning, deep flexion, or hip extension
- Observe for guarding behavior around the lower abdomen during exercises (protective movement patterns that suggest the student is managing scar sensitivity)
- Not pressure students to move through scar-adjacent discomfort
- Encourage students who have not done scar mobilization work to discuss this with their OB or midwife, and to seek a pelvic floor PT who addresses fascial scar work

### Return-to-Movement Timeline

The following is a general framework. Individual variation is significant, and any student with complications should be guided by their obstetric provider.

| Weeks Postpartum | What Is Generally Appropriate |
|-----------------|-------------------------------|
| 0–2 weeks | Rest; diaphragmatic breathing only; gentle walking |
| 2–4 weeks | Gentle walking; continued breathing work; light pelvic floor awareness (not loading) |
| 4–6 weeks | Short walks; gentle seated posture work; light supine breathing and TVA activation |
| 6–8 weeks | With OB/midwife clearance: begin DR-safe mat work; no exercises that load the incision site |
| 8–12 weeks | Progressive loading; introduce all positions gradually; begin prone work if comfortable |
| 12+ weeks | Continue progression; return to full repertoire as DR and pelvic floor function support |

Students are welcome to join class before they are cleared for full participation — they observe, connect with other mothers, and participate in the non-movement components (baby massage, music, closing meditation) until they are ready.

### Contraindications Specific to C-Section Recovery

- No prone positioning before six to eight weeks or before incision is fully healed without pain
- No loaded spinal flexion (sit-ups, roll-ups) before twelve weeks without PT clearance
- No heavy abdominal loading before fascial layer healing is confirmed
- No high-impact activity before twelve weeks
- No single-leg balance work until hip stability and core control are established
- Monitor for signs of hernia at the incision site (visible bulging during effort)

### Communication with OB/Midwife

Establish a referral relationship with obstetric providers in your area. When a student with a recent C-section joins your class, confirm that her provider has cleared her for group exercise. If unclear, draft a brief communication to the provider:

> *"[Student name] has enrolled in my postnatal Pilates class. The program includes mat-based exercise with low-impact movements, breath-coordinated core work, and infant developmental activities. I am aware of her recent cesarean and will follow a conservative return-to-movement protocol. Please let me know if there are specific movement restrictions I should observe, and whether you recommend any modification to her participation timeline."*

## Module 5: Breastfeeding and Movement

### Learning Objectives
By the end of this module, the student will be able to:
- Describe the effects of lactation hormones on connective tissue and energy availability
- Recognize and modify postural compensations associated with nursing
- Apply practical modifications for students who are actively breastfeeding

### Hormonal Considerations in Breastfeeding Women

Breastfeeding women present with a distinct hormonal profile that directly affects how they move, how they recover, and what their bodies need from exercise.

**Prolactin and relaxin:** Prolactin drives milk production and maintains joint laxity by sustaining relaxin levels. A woman who is six months postpartum and still breastfeeding may have the same degree of connective tissue laxity as she did at twelve weeks postpartum. Do not assume that time since birth is the only relevant variable — hormonal status matters equally.

**Oxytocin during exercise:** High-intensity exercise can temporarily suppress milk production and alter the taste of breast milk (due to elevated lactic acid in milk immediately post-exercise). This rarely affects class participation but is worth mentioning to students who are concerned about milk supply.

**Caloric and hydration demands:** A breastfeeding woman requires approximately 400–500 additional calories per day above her baseline. Combined with the caloric demands of exercise, this means many breastfeeding students are operating in a mild caloric deficit. Encourage hydration and pre-class snacking. Monitor for signs of lightheadedness, fatigue disproportionate to effort, or muscle cramping.

**Estrogen and collagen:** Low estrogen during breastfeeding impairs collagen synthesis. This affects the quality of tissue repair — including diastasis recti healing, scar remodeling, and pelvic floor recovery. Students who are breastfeeding may heal more slowly than formula-feeding counterparts, which is not a failure — it is a physiological reality.

### Postural Demands of Nursing

Nursing imposes a sustained postural load on the upper body that, without intervention, produces a characteristic pattern of dysfunction:

- **Forward head posture:** The head migrates anteriorly as the mother looks down at her nursing baby. For every inch the head moves forward of its neutral position, the effective weight of the head on the cervical spine increases by approximately 10 pounds.
- **Rounded shoulders and thoracic kyphosis:** The arms are held in front of the body for prolonged periods. The pectorals shorten. The rhomboids and middle trapezius lengthen and weaken.
- **Wrist and thumb strain:** Holding the baby's weight and supporting the breast places repeated stress on the wrist and thenar muscles, contributing to De Quervain's tenosynovitis.

**Class priorities for breastfeeding students:**
1. Anterior chain release and chest opening before loading
2. Thoracic mobility work integrated throughout session
3. Wrist modification options for all weight-bearing positions (fists, forearms)
4. Explicit cueing to the upper back (rhomboid engagement, shoulder blade setting) before and during pressing movements

### Practical Modifications

| Situation | Modification |
|-----------|-------------|
| Student arrives engorged | Offer prone modifications (Bolster or side-lying alternative for swan) |
| Student nurses during class | Provide pillow support; continue class without interruption; "feed your baby when your baby needs to eat — we will work around you" |
| Wrist pain | Forearm or fist plank; wrist wraps available in kit |
| Low energy | Reduce range, reduce load; prioritize breath and presence over intensity |

### Hydration Protocol

Water should be accessible and visible throughout class. The instructor models hydration. Students with breastfeeding infants are reminded at the beginning of each session to have water on their mats.

## Module 6: Biomechanics of Daily Motherhood and Anterior Chain Work

### Learning Objectives
By the end of this module, the student will be able to:
- Identify the postural and mechanical stressors most common in daily caregiving tasks
- Teach functional movement patterns that protect maternal joints and spine
- Apply anterior chain release techniques for the chest, anterior shoulder, and axillary complex
- Use the Bolster for prone anterior abdominal repositioning

### The Biomechanics of Daily Caregiving

Motherhood is physically demanding in ways that traditional fitness programming does not address. The postpartum Pilates instructor must understand not only what happens in class but what happens in the twenty-three hours when the student is not in class.

**Baby-carrying posture:** Carrying an infant on one hip — the characteristic postpartum stance — loads the spine asymmetrically and chronically. The hip shifts laterally to support the baby's weight, the ipsilateral quadratus lumborum shortens, the contralateral hip abductors overwork, and the lumbar spine develops a lateral tilt that, over months of repetition, can become a structural pattern. Alternate carrying sides. Cue this at every session.

**Lifting mechanics:** Mothers lift their babies from cribs, car seats, floor, and bath surfaces dozens of times per day — often at awkward heights and from non-neutral spine positions. Teach the mechanics explicitly:

- **From the floor:** Squat or deep lunge; maintain neutral spine; engage TVA before lifting; exhale on the lift
- **From the crib:** Bend at the hips and knees; do not round the thoracic spine over the crib rail; bring the baby close before straightening
- **From the car seat:** Face the car seat rather than twisting; use a squat, not a forward bend; avoid any rotation under load

**Diaper change mechanics:** The changing table height matters. Too low forces prolonged lumbar flexion. Cue students to adjust the table height or use the floor with a squat/kneeling position.

### The Anterior Chain: A Critical and Overlooked System

The anterior fascial line — running from the dorsum of the foot up through the shin, the quadriceps and rectus abdominis, the pectoralis major and minor, the sternocleidomastoid — is systematically compressed and shortened in new mothers.

The **axillary (armpit) complex** deserves particular attention because it is almost universally compromised and almost universally overlooked in postpartum programming.

The muscles that converge in and around the axilla include:
- **Pectoralis minor** — depresses the shoulder girdle; pulls the scapula forward and down; shortened by all forward-reach activity, including nursing
- **Serratus anterior** — protracts the scapula; overworked in holding-a-baby position
- **Subscapularis** — medially rotates the shoulder; shortened by chronic internal rotation during baby-holding
- **Axillary lymph nodes** — not a muscle, but clinically relevant: lymphatic drainage in this area is compromised by poor posture, tight musculature, and the postural habit of holding the arms close to the body

**Why this matters for your students:**
The combination of pectoralis minor shortness, subscapularis tightness, and serratus imbalance produces a shoulder complex that is internally rotated, protracted, and unstable — exactly the opposite of what is needed for safe overhead pressing, plank positions, or baby-holding. Before loading the upper body in any class, this tissue must be addressed.

### Anterior Chain Release Techniques

These techniques are appropriate to teach in class as part of the warm-up sequence. They take three to five minutes and yield significant downstream benefit to the quality of the Pilates work that follows.

**Chest and anterior shoulder release (standing or seated):**
1. Clasp hands behind the lower back or at the sacrum.
2. Inhale to lift the sternum toward the ceiling.
3. Draw the shoulder blades down and together on the exhale — do not collapse the lower back.
4. Hold for three to five breaths. Release and repeat.

**Pectoralis minor release (self-massage or partner):**
1. Locate the pectoralis minor by pressing gently under the clavicle, approximately two inches medial to the coracoid process.
2. Apply sustained gentle pressure with two fingers.
3. Breathe slowly while maintaining pressure. The muscle will release under the fingers over one to two minutes.
4. Follow with an arm-across-body stretch to lengthen the tissue after release.

**Axillary/subscapularis release:**
1. Reach one arm overhead.
2. Use the opposite hand to apply gentle sustained pressure into the axilla (armpit) — moving anteriorly to reach the subscapularis and posterior to access the teres muscles.
3. Maintain pressure for one minute per side.
4. Follow with external rotation range of motion (shoulder circles, goalpost position).

**Serratus anterior activation:**
1. In a four-point kneeling or plank position, practice "serratus push-ups" — small protraction/retraction of the shoulder blades while keeping the elbows straight.
2. This re-educates the serratus anterior after the prolonged holding-posture inhibition of early motherhood.
3. Three sets of ten is sufficient for activation in a warm-up context.

### The Bolster: Prone Anterior Abdominal Repositioning

**Setup:**
1. Place the Bolster transversely across the mat, positioned at the level of the anterior superior iliac spines (ASIS) — the "hip points."
2. The student lies prone over the Bolster, with the pelvis supported on the Bolster and the lower abdomen making contact.
3. Legs are extended on the mat behind. Arms are relaxed either forward or at the sides.
4. The student breathes slowly and allows body weight to settle into the Bolster over three to five minutes.

**Mechanism:**
The gentle, sustained pressure of body weight through the Bolster provides proprioceptive input to the anterior abdominal wall and pelvic contents. This encourages the organs and fascial structures that shifted anteriorly and inferiorly during pregnancy to begin repositioning toward their pre-pregnancy orientation. The effect is cumulative over multiple sessions.

**Contraindications:**
- Cesarean section before twelve weeks postpartum or before incision is fully healed
- Active pelvic pain
- Any reported discomfort with prone positioning
- Prolapse symptoms until cleared by pelvic floor PT

**Integration into class:**
The Bolster is used during the swan/cobra prep segment of the mat sequence. It can also be used during the opening infant massage segment for mothers who have already completed the prone contraindication screening.

## Module 7: Core Rehabilitation Progressions

### Learning Objectives
By the end of this module, the student will be able to:
- Apply a breath-first approach to postpartum core work
- Progress students through the full core rehabilitation continuum
- Identify when a student is ready for each stage of progression
- Integrate pelvic floor, diaphragm, TVA, and global stabilizer training

### The Breath-First Principle

In conventional Pilates, we teach breathing as a tool for rhythm, oxygenation, and release. In postpartum Pilates, breathing is the intervention itself.

The diaphragm and pelvic floor function as a coordinated pressure-management system. On inhalation, the diaphragm descends and the pelvic floor eccentrically lowers. On exhalation, the diaphragm ascends and the pelvic floor recoils. This reciprocal rhythm was disrupted during pregnancy — the growing uterus held the pelvic floor in a chronically loaded position and limited diaphragmatic excursion — and it must be restored before any meaningful core loading can occur safely.

Begin every postpartum core session with diaphragmatic breathing. This is not a warm-up placeholder. It is the foundation of everything that follows.

### Progression Framework

**Stage 1: Breath and Awareness (Weeks 0–6, or until cleared by provider)**

- Diaphragmatic breathing in supine (90-90 position)
- Gentle pelvic floor awareness (connect to the exhale without gripping)
- TVA activation: "draw hip bones gently toward each other; allow navel to soften toward spine"
- No loading; no repetitions; pure neuromuscular re-education

**Stage 2: Supine Foundation (Weeks 6–12 with clearance)**

- TVA holds during breathing (progressive breath holds)
- Heel slides (bilateral and alternating)
- Dead bug (modified — single limb, short lever)
- Bridges (double-leg; progress to single-leg only after bilateral is controlled)
- Pelvic tilts
- Side-lying hip abduction and clamshells

**Stage 3: Seated and All-Fours (Weeks 8–16 or when Stage 2 is controlled)**

- Quadruped bird-dog
- Serratus activation (quadruped protraction/retraction)
- Seated TVA activation with arm raises
- Modified cat-cow with breath coordination

**Stage 4: Standing and Loaded (Weeks 12+ or when Stage 3 is controlled)**

- Standing squats with TVA engagement
- Lunges with core control
- Lateral band walks (if bands are available)
- Wall plank progressing to incline plank progressing to forearm plank

**Stage 5: Full Pilates Repertoire (With clearance and functional midline tension)**

- Full plank
- Roll-ups
- The Hundred
- Double-leg work
- Rotation exercises (Criss Cross, Saw, Open Leg Rocker)
- Swan
- Return to all studio and class-level repertoire

### Integration of Pelvic Floor, Diaphragm, and TVA

The three-system coordination (diaphragm + pelvic floor + TVA) is not automatic in postpartum women. It must be explicitly taught and reinforced at every stage of progression.

**Cueing sequence for any loaded exercise:**
1. "Breathe in to prepare."
2. "On your exhale, gently lift the pelvic floor and draw the lower belly in."
3. "Hold that connection and move."

This sequence — sometimes called the "exhale, lift, and load" — is the cornerstone of safe postpartum Pilates instruction. Use it consistently until it becomes an internalized habit in your students.

### Progressive Loading Table

| Stage | Position | Exercise Examples | Load Level |
|-------|---------|-----------------|-----------|
| 1 | Supine | Breath, TVA activation | Neurological only |
| 2 | Supine | Heel slides, dead bug, bridges | Low load, single limb |
| 3 | Quadruped/Seated | Bird-dog, serratus, seated arm raises | Low-moderate |
| 4 | Standing | Squats, lunges, wall plank | Moderate |
| 5 | Full range | Pilates repertoire, rotation, dynamic | Full load, gated by function |

## Module 8: Infant and Toddler Development Foundations

### Learning Objectives
By the end of this module, the student will be able to:
- Explain the developmental rationale for each element of the class format
- Identify approximate motor, sensory, social, and language milestones by age
- Select and adapt class activities appropriate for the age range present
- Teach basic baby sign language to class participants
- Deliver the baby massage sequence with narrated developmental framing

### Visual Development

Newborns are not visually passive — they are actively learning to see. At birth, visual acuity is approximately 20/400. Infants can see best at a distance of 8 to 12 inches — roughly the distance between a nursing baby's face and the mother's face. Color perception is limited; the visual system is most sensitive to high-contrast patterns.

**The high-contrast cards in the Instructor Kit are developmentally intentional, not decorative.** Black and white geometric patterns at close range provide the maximum visual stimulation for infant cortical development in the first months of life. When instructors place these cards near infants in the center of the circle, they are offering appropriate visual enrichment during the periods when babies are alert but not yet mobile.

| Age | Visual Milestone | Class Application |
|-----|-----------------|------------------|
| 0–2 months | High contrast at 8–12 in; faces preferred | High-contrast cards; mom's face during exercises |
| 2–4 months | Color vision developing; tracking objects | Slow-moving scarves; colorful parachute |
| 4–6 months | Full color; depth perception emerging | Bubbles; mid-range visual targets |
| 6–9 months | Reaching guided by vision | Object permanence games; reaching for scarves |
| 12+ months | Near-adult acuity; visual memory | Puppet games; song visual associations |

### Auditory Development

Hearing is functional from approximately 16 weeks gestation — babies are born already familiar with their mother's voice and the auditory landscape of the womb. In class:

- **Rattles and shakers** stimulate auditory development through cause-and-effect exploration (infant shakes the rattle; sound follows)
- **Music** activates neural pathways associated with language, rhythm, and emotional regulation
- **The instructor's voice** is a constant developmental input — narrate, sing, and respond to babies' vocalizations throughout class

**Sound safety note:** Class instruments should be used to produce comfortable sound levels. No noisemakers should produce more than 80 dB at close range. All instruments in the Instructor Kit meet this standard.

### Social and Emotional Development

- **Puppet interaction (2–6 months):** Babies respond to puppet faces similarly to human faces in the early months. Use puppets at face level; move them slowly; allow babies to reach for and grasp them.
- **Eye contact and mirroring (0–12 months):** Reciprocal gaze between mother and baby is the earliest form of social interaction and the foundation of attachment. Class exercises that position mother and baby facing each other — chest press, lunges, plank with kisses — support this connection.
- **Parallel play (12–24 months):** Toddlers in this range play alongside but not yet with other children. The center circle arrangement supports this — each child has their own sensory experience within a shared space.
- **Cooperative play (24–36 months):** Older toddlers begin to engage with class activities as a group — parachute, scarf sharing, puppet games.

### Motor Milestones by Age

These milestones guide which exercises are safe with baby-as-prop and how the play center is configured.

| Age | Motor Milestone | Class Implications |
|-----|----------------|-------------------|
| 3 months | Lifts head in prone; responds to sound | Can be held in supported chest press; prone time on mat |
| 6 months | Sits with support; rolls; grasps objects | Can sit propped on mom's shins; reaches for objects |
| 9 months | Sits independently; begins pulling to stand | Can be seated between mom's feet in bridge; scarf play |
| 12 months | Pulls to stand; first steps | Mobile in center; gate the perimeter carefully |
| 18 months | Walking; beginning to run | Active participation in Freeze Dance; parachute |
| 24 months | Running; climbing; 2-word phrases | Full parachute participation; song following |
| 36 months | Jumping; 3–5 word sentences | Beginning Level II curriculum activities |

### Baby Sign Language

Pre-verbal communication reduces frustration for infants and toddlers who have language comprehension before language production. Baby sign language also deepens the bond between mother and child and adds an educational element that differentiates the class.

Teach the following signs in class — introduce one to two new signs per cohort session, building on prior ones:

| Sign | When to Introduce | Application in Class |
|------|------------------|---------------------|
| MORE | Session 1 | "Do you want MORE?" during massage |
| ALL DONE | Session 1 | End of massage; end of parachute |
| MILK | Session 2 | Nursing acknowledgment; moms model |
| MUSIC | Session 3 | Musical activities |
| UP | Session 3 | Lifting movements |
| BALL | Session 4 | Sensory ball play |
| PLEASE | Session 5 | Reinforces interaction |
| THANK YOU | Session 5 | Reinforces interaction |

Introduce the sign, model it while saying the word aloud, and encourage mothers to use it at home. Repeat previously taught signs at the beginning of each session.

### Baby Massage: Developmental Rationale

Baby massage is one of the most evidence-supported interventions available in an infant wellness context. Research demonstrates:

- **Weight gain in preterm infants:** Tactile stimulation in NICU settings has been shown to increase weight gain rates significantly
- **Improved sleep:** Massage before bedtime reduces cortisol, increases melatonin, and improves sleep duration
- **Reduced colic:** Abdominal strokes following the direction of digestion reduce gas and colic symptoms
- **Enhanced attachment:** Skin-to-skin touch with eye contact reinforces the mother-infant bond and co-regulates both nervous systems
- **Developmental stimulation:** Proprioceptive input through massage supports body-schema development — the infant's emerging sense of where their body is in space

The massage closing ritual is not an add-on. It is clinically grounded and developmentally essential. Never omit it.

**Massage Stroke Sequence (narrated by instructor):**

Instructors narrate each stroke and its purpose as they demonstrate. Parents follow in real time.

1. **Welcome stroke:** Both hands, full-body sweeping stroke from shoulders to feet. "This tells your baby that massage is beginning."
2. **Legs and feet (Indian and Swedish strokes):** Long strokes down each leg; foot circles; toe flexion and extension. "Stimulates proprioception; activates digestion."
3. **Abdomen (clockwise circles):** Follow the direction of peristalsis. "Supports gas and colic relief; stimulates digestion."
4. **Chest (butterfly stroke):** Both hands flat on chest, sweep outward like butterfly wings. "Opens the chest; deepens breathing."
5. **Arms and hands:** Long strokes; hand circles; finger extension. "Fine motor stimulation; releases grip reflex."
6. **Back (long strokes):** Baby prone; long strokes down either side of spine (never directly on the spine). "Activates parasympathetic nervous system; supports sleep."
7. **Face (small circles):** Forehead, temples, cheeks, jaw. "Releases facial tension; especially helpful for babies with nursing challenges."
8. **Closing stroke:** Full-body sweep, as in the welcome stroke. "Signals that massage is ending."

## Module 9: Psychological and Emotional Dimensions

### Learning Objectives
By the end of this module, the student will be able to:
- Identify the spectrum of postpartum mood disorders and their distinguishing features
- Apply a self-report screening tool without performing clinical assessment
- Create a physically and emotionally safe class environment
- Articulate clear referral pathways for mental health support
- Support maternal body image and postpartum identity without pathologizing or bypassing

### Postpartum Mood Disorders: What You Need to Know

The fitness instructor is often one of the first consistent points of social contact for a new mother — and this creates a meaningful (and bounded) opportunity for support. Understanding postpartum mood disorders helps instructors create a safe environment, respond appropriately to distress, and refer when needed.

**Postpartum Depression (PPD):**
Affects approximately 10–15% of new mothers (some studies put the figure higher). Symptoms include persistent sadness, loss of interest, fatigue beyond expected, feelings of worthlessness or guilt, difficulty bonding with baby, and thoughts of harming oneself or the baby. PPD can onset at any point in the first year — not only immediately after birth.

**Postpartum Anxiety (PPA):**
Affects up to 20% of new mothers and is frequently underdiagnosed because it presents differently from depression. Symptoms include excessive worry, inability to rest even when baby is sleeping, physical manifestations (racing heart, dizziness, nausea), intrusive thoughts about harm coming to the baby, and hypervigilance. PPA can coexist with PPD or present independently.

**Postpartum Rage:**
Less commonly named but commonly experienced. Disproportionate anger, irritability, and explosive frustration — often triggered by the relentlessness of caregiving, loss of self, and chronic sleep deprivation. Postpartum rage frequently signals underlying depression or anxiety and warrants the same referral response.

**Postpartum PTSD:**
Develops in response to a traumatic birth experience. Symptoms parallel PTSD in other contexts: flashbacks, hypervigilance, avoidance of reminders of the birth, emotional numbing, nightmares. This is clinically significant and requires professional support. Instructors should be aware that certain exercises (especially those involving supine positions or abdominal compression) may trigger avoidance or distress in women with birth trauma.

**Postpartum Psychosis:**
Rare (1–2 per 1,000 births) and a psychiatric emergency. Characterized by rapid onset of hallucinations, delusions, confused thinking, and erratic behavior. This requires emergency intervention — not a referral handout.

### Screening Without Diagnosing

The Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale (EPDS) is a validated 10-item self-report screening tool used in obstetric and primary care settings. Pilates & Mommy Certified Instructors may include the EPDS as part of the intake process as an informational self-report only — instructors are not qualified to interpret, score, or act upon EPDS scores clinically.

The purpose of including the EPDS in your intake packet is:
1. To normalize the conversation about postpartum mental health
2. To signal to students that this is a space where those experiences are acknowledged
3. To give students language for what they may be experiencing

At intake, instructors may say:

> *"There's a short self-report questionnaire about mood in your intake packet. It's not something I'll be reviewing or scoring — it's just there to encourage you to pay attention to how you're feeling and to start a conversation with your provider if anything resonates."*

### Creating Emotional Safety in the Movement Space

The Mommy & Me class environment must be explicitly designed to feel safe for mothers who may be struggling emotionally.

**Practices that build emotional safety:**
- Begin every session with an acknowledgment of the reality of new motherhood (without assuming difficulty): *"Whatever you're carrying in today, you're welcome to set it down here."*
- Never shame or compare. No leaderboards, no performance competition, no commentary on pace of recovery.
- Normalize nursing, crying (baby and mom), and stopping to soothe. These are not interruptions — they are the class.
- Build in community connection time. The circle setup and the shared snack/chat time before and after class create informal social bonding that may be the most important "intervention" of the week for an isolated new mother.
- Know your students by name. Know their babies by name. Use them.

**The instructor's role:** Hold space. Do not amplify. If a student discloses distress, listen without rushing to fix. Affirm. Refer when appropriate.

### When to Refer

**Refer to mental health resources when a student:**
- Discloses thoughts of harming herself or her baby (refer immediately and do not leave her alone)
- Reports that symptoms are interfering with daily functioning
- Describes persistent depression, anxiety, or rage beyond the first two weeks postpartum
- Indicates she has no social support or is experiencing partner conflict

**Referral language:**
> *"What you're sharing sounds really important, and I'm glad you felt safe enough to tell me. This is beyond what I can support in a fitness context, and I think you deserve more. Would you be open to me sharing some resources? Your OB is a great first call — they see this frequently and can connect you with someone."*

**Local resource bank:** Build a local referral list before your first cohort. Include:
- Postpartum Support International warmline: 1-800-944-4773
- Local pelvic floor physical therapists
- Therapists specializing in perinatal mental health
- OB/midwife practices with postpartum support protocols
- Lactation consultants

### Body Image and Postpartum Identity

The postpartum body is a changed body — and the cultural messaging around this change is relentlessly toxic. New mothers are bombarded with pressure to "get their body back," as though the body that grew and delivered a human being is an error to be corrected.

The Pilates & Mommy environment takes an explicit counter-cultural stance:

**This class is not about getting your body back. Your body did something extraordinary. This class is about reclaiming what your body can do.**

Instructor language matters:

- Do not cue toward aesthetic goals in class ("tighten that tummy," "flatten the belly")
- Do cue toward function and sensation ("feel the connection through the deep core," "sense how your breath supports that movement")
- Do not comment on participants' appearance, weight, or postpartum physical changes — not even positively ("you look great, can't even tell you just had a baby")
- Do celebrate physical accomplishments: strength, endurance, coordination, returning to an exercise they couldn't do last week

### Fighting the Hyper-Cathartic Nature of Motherhood

New motherhood produces a kind of emotional overwhelm that is often managed by one of two maladaptive extremes: suppression (everything is fine, I'm fine, the baby is fine) or catharsis (every feeling expressed in full, always). Neither extreme produces nervous system regulation — which is what mothers actually need.

The Pilates & Mommy class, when facilitated well, offers a third option: **regulated co-presence.** Mothers are held in a physical and relational space that is warm but structured, expressive but boundaried. They move their bodies, which discharges physical tension. They connect with other mothers, which reduces isolation. They are in the presence of their babies — which activates the oxytocin and attachment circuitry that, even on hard days, reminds them why this is worth it.

The instructor's role is to hold that container — not to process feelings in class, not to therapize, but to create the conditions where regulation can happen naturally through movement, music, breath, and community.

## Module 10: Building the Mommy & Me Class

### Learning Objectives
By the end of this module, the student will be able to:
- Set up a safe, complete Mommy & Me class environment
- Navigate the logistical realities of teaching mixed-age groups
- Manage the unpredictability inherent in infant and toddler participation
- Deliver the 90-minute arc with confidence and adaptability
- Implement the baby massage closing as a complete ritual

### Circle Setup Protocol

The circle setup is both functional and symbolic. It is not a preference — it is the format.

**Step-by-step setup:**

1. **Determine circle size:** Allow a minimum of 50 sq ft per dyad. For 8 participants, a circle approximately 20 feet in diameter.
2. **Lay the parachute:** Open the parachute in the center of the room. It will define the inner play area.
3. **Place mats:** Arrange mats around the perimeter of the parachute, facing inward. Each mat is one participant position. Space them equally.
4. **Set the play center:** Place sensory balls, stacking blocks, and textured balls on the parachute. Distribute high-contrast cards facing upward for any infants under 4 months.
5. **Check childproofing:** Walk the perimeter. All outlets covered. All hard objects out of reach. Doors managed. Flooring clear of trip hazards.
6. **Place class tools at instructor station:** Flow cards, music access, puppet, spare scarves, water.
7. **Greet families at the door:** First impressions of the space set the tone. Be ready before the first family arrives.

### Music Curation and Licensing

The music in a Mommy & Me class is not background — it is structural. It signals transitions, energizes movement, soothes, and provides the auditory scaffolding that makes the class feel like a complete experience.

**The music in the Instructor Kit** is pre-curated and licensed for instructor use in class settings. Do not substitute commercial streaming (Spotify, Apple Music, YouTube) without obtaining appropriate performance licenses, as this creates liability.

**Curation principles:**
- Uptempo nursery rhyme remixes for the mat work segment
- Recognizable melodies with modern production (parents enjoy this as much as babies)
- Softer versions (classical nursery rhyme arrangements, lullabies) for the closing segment
- "Farmer in the Dell" adapted with children's names: instructor cues this live or uses the recorded instrumental version and inserts names vocally

**Licensing note:** Public performance of recorded music requires a license from the performing rights organizations (ASCAP, BMI, SESAC) or use of pre-licensed content. The Instructor Kit playlist is pre-cleared. Instructors who build their own playlists must verify licensing independently.

### Age-Range Mixing

A typical class will include babies from 3 months to toddlers approaching 3 years. This age range is a feature, not a liability — older toddlers often provide a visual developmental model that younger babies track, and babies provide a grounding, slowing-down presence for busy toddlers.

**Managing the range:**

| Age Group | Primary Engagement | Instructor Attention Points |
|-----------|-------------------|----------------------------|
| 3–6 months | Visual tracking, tactile, held by parent | Ensure mom has support for holding positions; no floor-independent movement |
| 6–12 months | Object play, sensory balls, supported sitting | Mobile but not walking; monitor for tipping and falling in sitting |
| 12–18 months | Walking, cruising; exploring perimeter | Maintain circle boundary; redirect toward center; parachute holds attention |
| 18–24 months | Running, climbing, strong preferences | Toddler autonomy is high; offer choices; do not force participation in any activity |
| 24–36 months | Social play, following instruction, language | Engage in verbal instruction; assign "helper" roles in activities |

### Managing Unpredictability

The Mommy & Me class does not have a predictable flow. This is its nature, and the instructor who is at peace with this will teach well. The instructor who fights it will exhaust themselves.

**Crying babies:** Normal. Expected. The presence of crying does not mean the class is failing. Continue the class. Acknowledge the baby warmly ("baby is telling us something — let's keep moving while mama checks in"). Never create a culture of apologizing for infant needs.

**Wandering toddlers:** The parachute boundary and circle structure do most of the management work. When a toddler wanders, a light redirect ("come back to our circle!") and the lure of a puppet or scarf is usually sufficient. Do not stop the class.

**Nursing during class:** Fully normal and always welcome. Continue class; offer any necessary modifications for one-arm movements. Never create any pause or acknowledgment that makes the nursing mother feel she has disrupted anything.

**Two or more babies crying at once:** Pause the mat sequence. Move directly to a high-engagement activity (bubbles, parachute wave, puppet). Reset. Return to mat work when the room settles.

**A child who is consistently disruptive across multiple sessions:** Speak privately with the parent. The goal is not to exclude — it is to problem-solve together. Sometimes the child needs a different developmental stage class; sometimes the parent needs support strategies for managing their toddler in group settings; sometimes the fit of this particular cohort is not right.

### Baby Massage as Closing Ritual

The baby massage closing is not negotiable — it is the final chapter of every class and the element most often cited by mothers as their favorite.

**Framing for participants:**
> *"We're going to close the way we always do — with massage for your baby and a few minutes to breathe and settle. Follow along with me. I'll narrate what we're doing and why."*

**Instructor narration model:**
As you guide each stroke, briefly state its developmental purpose. This takes practice but becomes natural quickly.

> *"Long strokes down the legs — this stimulates proprioception, helping baby build a body map... Clockwise circles on the belly — this follows the direction of digestion and can help with gas and colic... The butterfly stroke on the chest opens the chest and deepens baby's breathing — notice how they settle into your hands..."*

**The Pat-a-Cake send-off:** Following the massage, the instructor leads one round of Pat-a-Cake as a playful bridge between the quiet massage state and the closing meditation. It signals that the deep quiet is over and the gentle transition out is beginning.

**Lullaby and meditation:**
The final ten minutes are intentionally slow. Choose a single consistent lullaby or closing song — one that families begin to associate with the end of class, so that in time, they can use it at home as a sleep and transition cue. Follow with a brief (3–5 minute) guided body scan and breath meditation for mothers.

### Appendix G: Baby Massage Stroke Guide

**PILATES & MOMMY — BABY MASSAGE STROKE GUIDE**

**Setup:** Baby on their back on a soft mat surface, in front of the parent. Warm hands by rubbing together. Ask the baby's permission: hold your hands above the baby and observe their response before beginning.

**Pressure:** Always light to moderate. Observe baby's cues — turning away, arching, crying indicate too much pressure or readiness to stop.

---

**Stroke 1 — Welcome Stroke**
Both hands placed at shoulders. Long, sweeping strokes from shoulders to feet, passing over torso and legs. Repeat three times. *Purpose: Signals beginning of massage; full-body proprioceptive input.*

**Stroke 2 — Indian Milking (Legs)**
Both hands wrapped around the thigh. One hand strokes from thigh to ankle while the other wraps the thigh. Alternate hands in a milking motion. Repeat ten times per leg. *Purpose: Stimulates lymphatic drainage; proprioceptive input to lower limbs.*

**Stroke 3 — Swedish Milking (Legs)**
Reverse of Stroke 2: strokes move from ankle toward the thigh. *Purpose: Venous return; warmth and circulation.*

**Stroke 4 — Foot Circles and Toe Work**
Support the foot with both hands. Thumbs draw small circles across the sole. Gently flex and extend each toe. *Purpose: Reflexology points; fine motor stimulation.*

**Stroke 5 — Abdomen (Clockwise Circles)**
One hand draws large clockwise circles on the abdomen, following the direction of the large intestine. *Purpose: Supports peristalsis; reduces gas and colic.*

**Stroke 6 — Sun-Moon Abdomen**
One hand makes a full circle (the "sun"); the other hand makes a half-circle (the "moon") on the opposite side. *Purpose: Continuous rhythmic abdominal contact; deepens digestive stimulation.*

**Stroke 7 — Butterfly Chest**
Both hands flat on the center of the chest. Stroke outward following the ribcage, like butterfly wings opening. *Purpose: Opens chest; deepens breath; stimulates intercostal muscles.*

**Stroke 8 — Cross-Heart Chest**
One hand strokes diagonally from lower left abdomen to right shoulder; alternate with the other hand from lower right to left shoulder. *Purpose: Cross-body integration; bilateral stimulation.*

**Stroke 9 — Arms and Hands**
Indian and Swedish milking on the arms, as on the legs. Follow with wrist circles and gentle finger extension. *Purpose: Releases grip reflex; fine motor stimulation.*

**Stroke 10 — Back (Baby Prone)**
Turn baby to prone if they tolerate it. Both hands stroke from the top of the back to the buttocks on either side of the spine (never directly on the spine). *Purpose: Activates parasympathetic nervous system; supports sleep.*

**Stroke 11 — Back Circles**
Small circles with fingertips across the back, from neck to sacrum. *Purpose: Deep relaxation; nerve pathway stimulation.*

**Stroke 12 — Face (Baby Supine)**
Return to supine. Small circles on the forehead, temples, cheeks, and jaw. Stroke outward from the nose across the cheeks. *Purpose: Releases facial tension; helpful for babies with nursing challenges or tight jaw.*

**Stroke 13 — Closing Stroke**
As the Welcome Stroke: long bilateral sweeping strokes from shoulders to feet. Repeat three times. *Purpose: Signals completion; full-body integration.*

### Appendix H: Parachute Games (Selected Activities)

**PILATES & MOMMY — PARACHUTE ACTIVITIES**

The parachute is used in the 1:00–1:10 segment. Choose three to four activities per session. Rotate through the list over the cohort.

### Appendix L: Toy Hygiene Protocol

**PILATES & MOMMY — TOY HYGIENE PROTOCOL**
*(30-Minute Sanitation Routine — Complete After Each Class)*

**Supplies needed:** Dish soap, warm water, clean towels, optional: fragrance-free baby-safe disinfectant spray

**Step 1 — Collection (5 minutes)**
Collect all class toys and instruments from the circle. Place in the provided bin or container. Note any items that are visibly soiled, broken, or need replacement.

**Step 2 — Rinse (5 minutes)**

**Step 3 — Wash (10 minutes)**
Wash all items with dish soap and warm water. Use a bottle brush for items with texture grooves. Pay special attention to stacking blocks (all surfaces) and rattle interiors (shake under running water).

**Step 4 — Disinfect (optional, 5 minutes)**

**Step 5 — Dry and Repack (5 minutes)**
Air dry on a clean towel or dry with a fresh towel. Repack into the Instructor Kit. Do not pack items that are still damp.

### Appendix M: Infant Visual Development Reference

**PILATES & MOMMY — INFANT VISUAL DEVELOPMENT REFERENCE**
*(High-Contrast Card Rationale by Age)*

The high-contrast black and white cards included in the Instructor Kit are not decorative. They are developmentally calibrated visual stimulation tools, grounded in the science of infant cortical development.

**The neurological rationale:**
The visual cortex is among the last cortical regions to fully myelinate. At birth, infants can resolve visual detail at approximately 20/400 — only high-contrast, large-feature patterns are detectable at close range. Providing appropriate visual stimulation during alert periods accelerates the development of the primary visual cortex, supports visual tracking, and lays the foundation for reading readiness.

| Age | Visual Capability | Appropriate Card Features | Class Use |
|-----|-----------------|--------------------------|----------|
| 0–6 weeks | High contrast only; 8–12 in range | Black/white geometric; bold lines; faces | Placed near mat within 12 in of baby |
| 6–12 weeks | Tracking developing; faces preferred | High contrast + simple face patterns | Hold cards above baby during mat work |
| 3–4 months | Color emerging; mid-range vision | Begin introducing color; simple patterns | Hang from parachute edge |
| 4–6 months | Depth perception; object permanence beginning | Objects with dimension; bold colors | Attach to pop-up toy; partial hide |
| 6+ months | Full color; visual memory | Picture cards; named objects | Naming games; "Where is the ball?" |
